ACT 01

The gate

Where cash enters the resort in its rawest form

A guest arrives at the barrier and pays KSh 3,000 to come in. They are handed a numbered wristband. That band is the only thing they need to carry for the rest of the day.

Here is the part that is easy to miss, and it is the cleverest thing in the system: the entry fee is not swallowed by the gate. It becomes credit. The guest has already got their KSh 3,000 back as spending money the moment they walked in. They can drink it, eat it, or spend it at the spa.

One record does both jobs — it settles the physical cash the gate must account for at closing, and it opens the guest's tab at KSh 3,000 in credit. There is no second entry to keep in step, so the two can never disagree.

A small sum worth doing in your head

On the screen above: 20 bands issued today, and entry revenue of KSh 60,000.

Twenty times three thousand is sixty thousand. Those two figures agree because the second one is not stored anywhere — it is worked out from the bands themselves, every time the screen is opened.

Nobody typed 60,000. Nobody can type 60,000. If someone issued a band without taking the money, this number could not quietly stay right.

And read the four bands listed above that total

Bands #18, #19 and #20 each show KSh 1,200 credit left. Band #17 shows KSh 4,200 owed.

Same list, same widget, opposite meanings — and it says which is which in words rather than showing a minus sign and hoping. Gate staff can tell at a glance who still has money on their band and who must settle before leaving.

Band #17 is in that state because it is the band this document tested against: four tilapia dishes were charged to it while proving the stock deduction in Act 3. Which is itself the point — the number moved because something really happened, and it is still moving as the resort is used.

A fault found by trying to photograph a screen

One page could not be captured, and chasing why turned up a real problem.

Asking the station tablet for the band-issue page returns a login screen. Not an error, not "page not found" — a login screen, as though the person had been signed out.

The cause is that the station app has no such page. Issuing a band happens on the gate screen shown above, with the button right there on it. But when the station app is asked for an address it does not recognise, anything it does not recognise, it shows the login screen as a catch-all.

So a mistyped address, a stale bookmark or an old link does not say "that page does not exist." It says, in effect, "sign in again" — sending staff to hunt for a session problem that is not there. It is also exactly how a previous attempt at this document ended up with eight screenshots of a login page filed under eight different dashboard names.

This has since been fixed, and the screen below is the result. An unrecognised address now says so, keeps the person signed in, and offers a way back — which matters on a fixed tablet with no browser buttons to escape with.

Anyone not signed in still goes to the login screen, exactly as before. Both halves are held in place by a test, because repairing one by breaking the other would be worse than the original fault.

Why band numbers cannot repeat

Two guests holding band #14 on the same day would make every bill after that meaningless. So numbers are handed out one at a time: while one is being allocated, every other request waits. The database refuses a duplicate even if the software somehow tried, which means a bug could not create one either.

Numbers restart each day, and the date is part of what makes them unique — so #14 on Monday and #14 on Tuesday are different bands and can never be confused.

The spending ceiling

A band can run to KSh 6,000 — double the entry fee — and no further. At that point staff are told, in plain words, to send the guest back to the gate to add credit.

This is the resort's main protection against walking losses. A guest cannot quietly run up an unlimited tab on a band and disappear; the exposure is capped at a number the owner chose.

ACT 02

One bill, payable anywhere

The band is the account number

The guest swims, orders a beer at the bar, eats at the restaurant, has a massage, takes a boat out. Five departments, five different members of staff, none of whom need to know what the others did.

Every one of those charges lands on the same tab, because the band number is the account number. The guest settles once, at the end, wherever they happen to be.

Nobody adds anything up by hand and nobody stores a running total. The balance is worked out fresh every time it is asked for. That is why the figure the bar sees and the figure the gate sees are always the same figure — there is only one, and it is calculated, not remembered.

The whole idea, in one input box

Above the treatments on the spa screen sits a single field: "Wristband # — charge to band credit."

That is the entire mechanism. The spa attendant does not take money, does not know what the guest spent at the bar, and does not open a bill. They type the band number. The charge joins everything else the guest has done today.

The same field appears at the bar, at the restaurant, at the boat jetty. One number, carried on the guest's wrist, and no cash changing hands in five different places.

The clearest picture in this document

Read the right-hand panel from the bottom up. Payments: CASH −KSh 3,000 — that is the entry fee, sitting on the tab as credit, exactly as Act 1 described. Charges: 1.00× Grilled Tilapia, KSh 1,800. And then Band credit left: KSh 1,200.

Three thousand paid, one thousand eight hundred spent, one thousand two hundred left. The guest can keep spending that anywhere on the property, and there is still only one bill.

Notice what is not there: no running total that someone maintains, no per-department bill to add up at the end, and no way to type over the balance. The 1,200 is the subtraction, performed at the moment you look at it.

The dish is also marked SERVED — the same event that moved 0.35 kg of fish out of the store room in Act 3. One action by the chef, three consequences: the guest is charged, the kitchen ticket clears, and the stock falls.

ACT 03

How the store room knows

The question that has no easy answer at a resort

A bar is easy. Sell a Tusker, remove a Tusker. A kitchen is harder but still manageable — a dish has a recipe, and the recipe says how much of each thing it eats.

The spa is where most systems quietly give up. A massage uses oil. How much? Nobody scans anything. The therapist does not weigh the bottle. And a boat ride burns fuel that no till ever sees.

This system answers it by refusing to guess. Every sellable thing must be one of exactly four kinds, and a thing nobody has classified cannot be sold at all.

Read the badges on that screenshot

Jet Ski Ride is a RECIPE — it burns fuel, so selling one deducts fuel. It also shows 50% cost, meaning half of the KSh 3,500 goes on what it consumes.

Kayaking, the pool day pass, hiking and the cycling tour are SERVICE — somebody confirmed these genuinely consume nothing.

Boat Ride, Fishing Trip and Golf Cart Ride are in red: NOT TRACKED — CANNOT SELL. All three burn fuel and nobody has said how much yet, so the system will not sell them.

That is the enforcement, printed on the row. Not a warning to be ignored — the item is simply unavailable until a human decides. The grey "Consumes nothing" button beside each one is the one-tap way to settle it, for the cases where that is the true answer.

Why the last box matters most

The difference between SERVICE and UNTRACKED is the difference between "a person checked, and this genuinely uses nothing" and "nobody has looked at this yet."

They would both deduct zero. Most systems treat them the same and silently leak stock forever. Here they are separate on purpose, and that separation is what makes it safe to block the sale — the system is not refusing because it is broken, it is refusing because a decision is genuinely missing.

The bug this document exists partly to record

Until recently, the spa leaked completely — and so did water sports, and every other department that hands something over immediately.

The reason was subtle. Stock was deducted at the moment the kitchen or bar marked an order ready. But a massage is never marked ready by anyone; it goes straight to served. So the deduction step was never reached, and the safety net that was supposed to warn about missing recipes sat behind the same door.

Everything that did not pass through a kitchen pass or a bar counter consumed nothing at all, and no warning was ever raised. It is fixed, and the figures below are from a run that proves it.

Watching it happen, once, on purpose

Claims like the ones above are cheap. So here is one sale, put through the running system while this document was being written, with the store room watched on either side of it.

The dish was a Grilled Tilapia at KSh 1,800. Its recipe says one portion takes 0.35 kg of tilapia fillet, along with oil, spices, onions and tomatoes.

The number that matters

7.2000 − 6.8500 = 0.3500 kg. Exactly the recipe line. Not approximately.

Nobody in the kitchen typed a stock figure. The chef marked a dish ready, which is the one thing a chef was going to do anyway, and the store room updated itself.

The deduction is also stamped with who — the ledger row reads Auto-consumption: order_item=… against the chef's name. So a variance at the end of the month can be walked back to individual dishes.

ACT 04

The guest leaves, the day closes

Where a system either catches a shortfall or hides it

At the barrier, the band is closed. Any credit the guest did not spend is forfeited — it becomes resort revenue. There is no refund path in the system, and that is a decision, not an omission: a refund at the gate is cash leaving the building at the least supervised moment of the day.

Bands that were never closed — the guest wandered out, staff got busy — are swept at end of day and forfeited together, so nothing stays open overnight quietly holding credit.

Counting the cash drawer

At the end of a shift the system already knows what should be in the drawer — it is the sum of every cash payment taken. Staff count what is actually there and enter it.

If the two disagree, the difference is recorded as a shortfall against the person who counted. It is not smoothed over and there is no button to make it balance.

Worth saying out loud before you go live

This strictness is correct, and it will hurt in week one. New staff make counting mistakes, and every one of them will be recorded against a real person's name.

Decide in advance what a first-week shortfall means, and tell staff before their first shift — not after their first mistake.
